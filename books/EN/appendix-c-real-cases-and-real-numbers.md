
# Appendix C --- Real Cases and Real Numbers

## Why Cases Matter

A book about the future runs a constant risk: it can float above the ground, all vision and no evidence. This appendix is the antidote. It gathers a handful of real, publicly documented industrial cases --- cases you can look up, read about, and check for yourself --- and reads each one through the lens of the sixth revolution. The point is not to claim that any of these companies uses this book's exact technology; the point is to show that the *pattern* this book describes is not speculative. The pattern --- a machine that perceives, a system that keeps the data, an intelligence that reasons, and a process that is automated --- is already being built, in pieces, by some of the most serious industrial organizations in the world. What the sixth revolution adds is the *connection* of those pieces into a single loop, and the deliberate choice to keep the data inside the company's own walls.

This appendix is honest about its sources. Each case is real and widely documented, but the specific figures associated with each are often disputed, reported differently by different sources, or simply not public. Where a number is cited, it is cited as *reported* or *often quoted*, not as a certified fact. Where a claim is illustrative, it is marked as such. The reader who wants the exact figures should follow the sources, and the honest reader will find that the *pattern* is more robust than any single number.

## Case One --- Rolls-Royce and the Engine That Sells Hours

Perhaps the most cited example of industrial servitization is **Rolls-Royce's "Power by the Hour"** model for jet engines. Instead of selling an engine outright, the company sells the *outcome* --- the hours of thrust the engine provides --- and keeps ownership of the engine and responsibility for its health. To make that model work, the company must know, continuously, how each engine is doing: its temperature, its vibration, its performance, its wear. The engines are instrumented, and their telemetry is monitored so that maintenance can be predicted and scheduled, rather than discovered when an engine fails on a wing.

Read through the lens of this book, the case is almost a textbook illustration of the chain. The **machine** (the engine) is instrumented and perceives its own condition. The **telemetry** flows to a system that keeps it and reasons over it. The **agent** --- in Rolls-Royce's case, a team of engineers and analytics --- decides when maintenance is due and what it should be. And the **department** (maintenance, logistics, customer service) is coordinated around the prediction, so the aircraft is serviced on a schedule rather than an emergency. The sixth revolution's contribution is not the idea --- Rolls-Royce proved the idea decades ago --- but the *democratization* of it: what a giant aero-engine maker can afford to build with a specialist team, a small workshop can now build with a chip, a private cloud, and an agent.

## Case Two --- The Lights-Out Factory

There is a category of factory that runs with almost no human present: the "lights-out" plant, so automated that the lights can be turned off because no one needs to see. The Japanese robotics company **Fanuc** is the most often cited example; its plants are famous for running highly automated lines, with robots building robots, and for operating, in places, around the clock with minimal human supervision. The image is striking, and it is real --- but it is easy to misread. The lights-out factory is not a factory *without* people; it is a factory in which the people have been moved from the line to the *maintenance and oversight* of the line. The robots do the repetitive work; the humans keep the robots working, and judge when the line should stop.

Read through the lens of this book, the lights-out factory is a picture of the *machine that acts* (Part II) taken to its limit, and of the *human promoted to the conductor* (Part VI) made concrete. The robots perceive, decide, and act; the humans hold the veto, the judgment, and the responsibility. And the sixth revolution's private cloud and agent add the one thing the classic lights-out factory often lacked: a *coordinated memory* of what the whole plant is doing, kept safely, and reasoned over by an agent that knows the business. The lights-out factory is the sixth revolution's *physical* half; the private cloud and agent are its *nervous* half, and a plant that has both is the Industry 7.0 factory of Chapter 20.

## Case Three --- The Warehouse That Walks

The warehouse is where the most dramatic automation has already happened, and it is a case study in both the promise and the limit of the sixth revolution. **Amazon** acquired the robotics company Kiva Systems in 2012 and deployed, over the following years, tens of thousands of mobile robots that carry shelves to human pickers --- a system that dramatically reduced the walking, and the time, of order fulfilment. The robots are a clear case of the machine that perceives and acts: they sense their position, navigate the floor, and carry the goods.

But the most interesting case for this book is the *next* step. In 2023, Amazon began testing a humanoid robot --- **Agility Robotics' Digit** --- in its warehouses, for the repetitive tasks of moving totes and containers. Digit is not a wheeled cart; it is a biped, built to use the stairs, the aisles, and the hand-height of a world designed for humans. It is, in other words, exactly the humanoid of Chapter 21: a machine that walks onto the floor to carry the work that wears down the human body, rather than a machine built to redesign the floor. The warehouse that walks is the sixth revolution's humanoid thesis, already in a pilot, in a real building, documented in the public record.

Read through the lens of this book, the warehouse illustrates the whole chain at speed: the mobile robot and the humanoid are the *machine that perceives and acts*; the warehouse management system is the *memory*; the scheduling and routing intelligence is the *agent*; and the fulfilment, inventory, and customer-service departments are the *work that is automated*. The sixth revolution is not a future warehouse; it is a warehouse that already exists, and the question is only how far the loop will close.

## Case Four --- The Digital Twin

A "digital twin" is a live, digital model of a physical thing --- a machine, a line, a plant --- that mirrors its real state and can be used to predict, simulate, and optimize. The German industrial company **Siemens** has made the digital twin a centerpiece of its "digital factory" vision, and its electronics plant in Amberg, Germany, is one of the most frequently cited examples of a highly automated, digitally modelled manufacturing site; the plant is often described as running with the great majority of its processes automated, and with its products carrying their own digital identity through production.

Read through the lens of this book, the digital twin is the *memory* layer of the sixth revolution made visible. The machine perceives; the twin remembers; the twin can be used, by the agent, to reason about what will happen next. What the sixth revolution adds to the classic digital twin is *locality and privacy*: the twin of this book lives, in its most urgent form, *on the machine itself* (a local digital twin, Chapter 9), and its long memory lives in a private, zero-knowledge cloud *inside the company's walls* (Part III). The twin is not shipped to a public cloud to be modelled by a stranger; it stays home, where it belongs, and where it cannot be read by anyone the company has not chosen to trust.

## Case Five --- The Honest Failure

A book that only told success stories would be dishonest, and the sixth revolution has its failures too. The most instructive is **GE's Predix**, the industrial-internet platform that General Electric launched with great ambition in the mid-2010s, promising to connect industrial machines to a cloud and to transform how industry worked. The ambition was real and the technology was serious, but the execution struggled: the platform was complex, the business model was uncertain, and the company ultimately restructured the effort, with significant losses. The case is often cited, now, as a cautionary tale about the gap between an industrial-internet vision and a working, adopted product.

Read through the lens of this book, the GE case is a lesson in exactly the *productivity paradox* we met in Chapter 4, and in the *system is the product* principle. A brilliant platform, dropped into an old system, changes nothing --- because the value appears only when the whole loop closes, from the machine through the cloud through the agent to the department and back. GE built a magnificent piece of the loop (the cloud and the analytics) but the loop did not close in the customer's factory, and so the value did not appear. The lesson for the sixth revolution is the one this book keeps repeating: do not build the grand plan first; close one real loop, measure it, and extend it. The failure is not a reason to doubt the revolution; it is a reason to adopt it the disciplined way, one loop at a time.

## The Common Thread

Read together, these five cases teach a single, consistent lesson. The *components* of the sixth revolution are already proven: engines that report their health (Rolls-Royce), robots that build and carry (Fanuc, Amazon), plants that model themselves (Siemens), platforms that try to connect it all (GE). What has been missing --- and what the sixth revolution supplies --- is the *closed loop that stays inside the company*: the machine that perceives, the private cloud that keeps the secret, the agent that knows the business, and the departments that are automated, all connected and all private. Every one of these cases is a piece of that loop. None of them, on its own, is the loop. The sixth revolution is the moment the pieces are finally plugged into one another, and the moment the data is kept where it belongs.

## The Honest Caveat

Every case in this appendix is real and documented, but the specific numbers behind them are often reported, disputed, or private, and this appendix has been careful to say so. The reader should treat the cases as *evidence of the pattern*, not as audited data. The one claim this appendix makes without hedging is the one that matters: the components of the sixth revolution are already in the world, proven in real factories and real warehouses, and the only remaining question is how quickly and how safely the pieces are connected into the loop. That question is answered not by this book, but by the companies that choose to close their first loop --- and by the humans who choose to be its conductor.
