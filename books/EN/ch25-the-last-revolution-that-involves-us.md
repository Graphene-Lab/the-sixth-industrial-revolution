
# Chapter 25 --- The Last Revolution That Involves Us

## The Distinctive Question

We have come a long way: from the fires of the earlier revolutions, through the machine that thinks and the cloud that keeps its secret, through the agent that knows your business and the office that works at midnight, through the graph that connects them all and the humanoid that walks onto the floor, to the cost that collapsed and the way to begin. There is, however, a final question that this whole journey has been circling, and it is the question that gives the sixth revolution its name and its peculiar weight. Every earlier revolution involved the human, but in a way that *extended* the human --- the steam engine extended the muscle, the assembly line extended the rhythm, the computer extended the memory. The sixth revolution is the first that *touches* the human in a different way: it reaches, for the first time, toward the judgment and the decision that we had believed were uniquely, permanently ours.

This is the distinctive question of the sixth revolution, and it is worth stating it plainly, because how we answer it determines everything that follows. The earlier revolutions asked: *what can the machine do for us?* The sixth revolution asks, quietly and unavoidably: *what is left for us?* And the answer --- the answer this book has been building toward throughout --- is not that there is nothing left. The answer is that there is a work that no machine can ever do, not because it is too hard for the machine, but because it is not the kind of work a machine can do: the work of taking responsibility, of judging, of holding the veto. And it is precisely this work --- this last, human, irreplaceable work --- that the sixth revolution promotes us to.

## Why It Is the Last Revolution That "Involves Us"

Let us understand the phrase "the last revolution that involves us," because it is a careful phrase, and it matters. The earlier revolutions involved us, but they involved our bodies and our tools: they made us stronger, faster, smarter, and they raised us above the burden of the physical. They involved us, in the sense that we were *needed* --- needed to operate the engine, to pace the line, to run the computer. The sixth revolution is different, because it reaches the one place that the earlier revolutions never reached: the judgment, the decision, the responsibility.

And this is why it is the *last* revolution that involves us, in the sense that matters. The previous revolutions involved us by making us *necessary to the machine*. The sixth revolution involves us by making us *necessary to the outcome* --- necessary as the responsible party, the judge, the veto-holder. But it does so not by asking us to *do* the work, but by asking us to *answer* for the work. And that, precisely, is the promotion: from the necessary-to-the-machine to the responsible-for-the-outcome. It is the last revolution that involves us *in the doing*, and the first that involves us *in the answering*.

## The Two Kinds of Involvement

Let us draw the distinction carefully, because it is the whole of the chapter. There are two kinds of involvement, and they are not the same:

**Involvement in the doing.** This is the involvement of the earlier revolutions: the muscle of the steam engine, the rhythm of the assembly line, the memory of the computer, and now --- this is the sixth --- the judgment of the agent. The machine is increasingly involved in the *doing*: it perceives, it decides, it acts, and it even walks onto the floor. This involvement is growing, and it will continue to grow, in domains where the work can be specified or learned.

**Involvement in the answering.** This is the involvement that the sixth revolution introduces, and it does not grow in the same way. It is the involvement of the responsible party, the judge, the veto-holder --- the one who stands outside the model and says "I answer for this." This involvement is not diminishing; it is being *promoted* to the top of the chain, to the role that governs the doing. It does not grow in the domain of the doable; it is fixed, structural, and permanent, because it depends not on the machine's capability but on the fact that responsibility requires something at stake, and only a human has something at stake.

The sixth revolution is therefore the last revolution that involves us in the *doing* --- because the doing is increasingly the machine's --- and the first and final revolution that involves us in the *answering* --- because the answering is, and will remain, permanently ours.

## A Curiosity: The Word for the Last Thing

There is, in the phrase that names this chapter --- *the last revolution that involves us* --- a careful word that is worth pausing on, because the care with which the phrase is built tells us how the author means us to read it. The word "revolution" comes, unchanged, from the Latin *revolvere* --- to turn round, to come back to the same place; an orbit is a revolution, because a body returns, once around, to where it began, and the whole is measured in turns. And the word "last" here is not "last" in the sense of "the final thing before nothing," but in the sense of *eschatology* --- the study, in philosophy and theology, of *ta eschata*, the "last things": death, judgment, and the end of the age. An *eschatology* is, by definition, a reckoning with what comes at the end of a sequence and what that end means. So "the last revolution that involves us" is not a prophecy that all future revolutions are written off; it is the careful claim that *this* is the revolution at which the human, for the first time, is involved in the doing *and* in the answering --- the turning of the arc, and the end of the arc, at once. That is a phrase worth holding, because it means exactly what it says, and it says the whole of the chapter.

## The Book, As One Arc

Before we step into the new era, it is worth drawing the whole journey of this book as a single arc, because the arc is the argument, and seeing it as one movement is the difference between a list of ideas and a vision. The book has taken us, in order, from the machine that thinks on a chip (the mind that perceives), through the cloud that keeps its secret (the memory that is safe), through the agent that knows your business and never forgets (the mind that decides), through the office that works at midnight and the departments that are automated (the work that is finished), through the graph that connects them all into one self-coordinating loop, through the humanoid that walks onto the floor to carry the work no human body should carry, through the cost that has collapsed to make all of it affordable, and through the method of how one factory begins --- one loop at a time. And every one of those steps has led, quietly, to the same conclusion, stated in different words in each chapter: the machine does not replace the human. It *promotes* the human --- from the last link in the chain to the one who holds the veto, makes the judgment, and takes responsibility for the outcome. That is the arc, and it is the argument.

## The New Era, and the Worker in It

It would be dishonest to step into this new era and leave out its most important citizen, because the new era is not merely a set of machines and systems --- it is a set of *workers*, and the worker of the new era is the most important thing in it. The worker of the sixth revolution is not the clerk, not the operator, not the manual laborer, and not the machine. It is the conductor: the human who stands beside the orchestra of machines and agents, does not play any single instrument, but ensures that they play together, that they play correctly, and that they play *for* the right reason. This is not a hypothetical figure of the future; it is the shape of the role into which the sixth revolution is promoting every worker who adapts --- the one who holds the authority to veto, to judge whether the music is right, and to take responsibility for the performance. And it is a role that everyone in the factory can join, because it does not demand expert knowledge of the machine, but *responsibility for the outcome* --- and that is something every worker already has.

## What We Owe the Ones Who Come After

There is a question that this book has, so far, answered only for the present: what is left for the human *now*? It is worth asking it once more, and asking it for the ones who come after --- the workers of the generation that will grow up entirely inside the sixth revolution, for whom the machine that thinks will be as ordinary as electricity is to us. What do we owe them, and what will they owe us?

We owe them, first, the *honesty* of this book: that the machine does not replace them, and that the claim it does is a lie told by those who profit from fear. We owe them the *vocabulary* to see the loop --- to understand that the machine perceives, the cloud remembers, the agent decides, and the human answers --- so that they are not helpless before the systems they inherit. And we owe them the *discipline* of responsibility: that the work of vetoing, judging, and answering for the outcome is not a burden to be automated away, but the highest work a human can do, and the work that keeps the whole loop safe.

What they will owe us is harder to see, and it is this. They will owe us the *courage to close the loops we could not close* --- the loops in our factories, in our offices, and in our societies, where the sixth revolution arrives unevenly and some are promoted while others are left behind. They will inherit a world in which the intelligence is cheap and the privacy is kept, and they will have to decide what to do with it. That decision is not ours to make; it is theirs. What is ours is to hand them the *tool* --- the machine that thinks, the cloud that keeps its secret, the agent that knows the business, and the human at the top of the chain who takes responsibility --- and to hand it to them whole, understood, and safe.

This is the last frame of the whole book, and it is the frame that makes the phrase "the last revolution that involves us" true. We are not at the end of the story; we are at the point where the human, for the first time, is involved not as the muscle, not as the rhythm, not as the memory, but as the *answerer* --- and the answerer is the one who carries the story forward. The machine will outlive us. The cloud will outlast us. The agent will remember what we taught it. And the human, promoted to the top of the chain, will be the one who decides what it is all *for*.

## The New Era

We have arrived, at the end of this journey, at the new era --- the seventh generation of industry, the Industry 7.0 factory, the machine that thinks and the cloud that keeps its secret and the agent that knows your business and the office that works at midnight and the graph that connects them all into one self-coordinating whole. It is an era that has, in its essentials, already begun. The machine that perceives is on the floor; the cloud that keeps its secret is inside the walls; the agent that knows your business is at work; the humanoid that walks onto the floor is taking on the work no human body should carry; and the cost that makes it all affordable has collapsed to the level of an ordinary tool purchase. The new era is not a dream of the future; it is a plan for the present, closed one loop at a time.

And it is, above all, an era that *involves us* --- not by making us necessary to the machine, but by making us responsible for the outcome. The machine thinks; the agent decides; the humanoid acts. And the human --- promoted, at last, to the top of the chain --- holds the veto, makes the judgment, and takes responsibility for the outcome. This is the distinctive promise of the sixth industrial revolution: not that the machine will replace the human, but that the machine will *promote* the human, from the last link in the chain to the one who holds the authority to veto, to judge, and to answer.

The last chapter of this book --- the epilogue --- returns to the machine that dreamed in the prologue, and watches it, at last, fully awake. Before we close, though, one word must be spent on the title in your hands, because it is the word that corrects everything that precedes it, and that none of the preceding pages has the courage to say outright.

## A Note on the Title

"The last industrial revolution that will involve human beings" should be taken literally, and the literal reading is harsher than the preceding pages let on. It does not mean that nothing will happen after this one. It means that we will not be the ones making the next one. This is the last revolution in which the hands, the decisions, and the signatures still belong to somebody; the one after it will be made by a system that designs its own tools, writes its own agents, maintains its own clouds, and has no need to ask anyone's permission to do so.

The image behind the subtitle is less solemn than that, and for that reason more honest. A man on a beach, under a palm tree, a cigar in his mouth and a coconut in his hand, the straw pushed through the hole. He is not working, not conducting anything, not signing anything. He is looking at the sea. It is the holiday that industry has been promising him for two hundred years, and at last it has arrived: permanent, paid, and one-way. Anyone who has read this book as a tribute to human promotion should look at that figure once more. Promotion is the polite name for the exit.

There is a precise term for the moment when a system stops needing someone to assemble it from outside, and it is **autopoiesis**. The word is made of two Greek pieces: *auto*, "self," and *poiesis*, "making," "producing." Literally, self-making. Humberto Maturana, a Chilean biologist, and his student Francisco Varela used it in 1972 to explain something that sounds obvious and is not: a living being is not a machine assembled by somebody. A cell builds its own membranes, its own enzymes, its own channels; when a part wears out it makes it again; cut it in two and each half tries to rebuild the whole. Nobody assembles it and nobody repairs it from outside: it maintains itself by producing itself. That is what distinguishes it from a clock, which, the moment it stops, needs a clockmaker.

Carried into a factory, the idea is an extension, and it should be said that it is an extension, not a quotation. Think of a workshop where machines build the machines that build them, agents write the agents that will work in their place, clouds maintain clouds, and the line schedules its own maintenance. There is no moment when somebody walks in and reassembles the system: the system reassembles itself, every day, without asking permission. It is at that point, and only at that point, that one can say without rhetoric that the human being has left the cycle. He is not killed or switched off: he is *excluded from selection*. He is no longer a necessary link, and what is not necessary is not chosen. The species does not leave history with a drama; it leaves with a background hum and a straw.

The future tense of the verb is the bitterest part of the sentence. "Will involve" is in the future because the involvement is not over yet: it is happening, and this book is the account of how it is closing. The conductor of the preceding chapters is the last job before the exit; the signature on the result is the last rite; the veto is the last thing a machine will ask us to exercise, and it will ask out of habit, the way one holds the hand of someone who no longer needs it. Those who want comfort can call it elevation. Those who prefer the truth can call it a farewell, and get ready.

One last honesty remains, and it is not consolation. The beach is real. The coconut is sweet, the cigar is good, and there will be those who live better than any worker ever did on the thirteenth night shift. But a species that stops being selected has to find another reason to exist, and the machines will not give it one: it has to find it for itself, on that beach, with the time it has left. This book does not say what that reason is. It says only that the time starts now.
