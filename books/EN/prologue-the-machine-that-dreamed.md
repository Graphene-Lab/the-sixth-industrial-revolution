
# Prologue --- The Machine That Dreamed

It is four in the morning on a plant somewhere out of the city, and the day shift has not yet come through the turnstiles. The building breathes the way all large buildings breathe when they are asleep --- a low hum of compressors, the click of a relay settling, the sigh of ventilation moving air that no longer needs warming. On line three a robotic arm is completing its forty-thousandth weld of a running month, and it does so without being told to, because its instructions were written into its bones the day it was switched on.

This is where the story begins, not at its loudest but at its quietest.

A sensor the size of a coin is pressed against the spindle of a milling machine. It is not the kind of sensor that merely counts revolutions and sends a number to a screen for a man to glance at while his coffee cools. It is a small computer. Inside it, a neural processor the width of a fingernail is asking the machine a question in a language the machine did not know it could speak: *how are you feeling?* And the machine answers, in the only way a machine can, by letting the shape of its vibration be read. The vibration says *a little lonely today, a bearing near the front is beginning to complain*. The little computer files this not as a number but as a feeling-with-a-number, and sends it into the dark.

It sends it into a cloud. Not the cloud of postcards and advertising, the one that watches through the window, but a cloud kept on the other side of a wall the company owns. The data does not leave the building except as a whisper that is encrypted before it is spoken and decrypted only where it must be heard. It arrives, and somewhere in the building a second intelligence --- one that has been reading the whole plant's telemetry for a year, that knows the difference between the sound of a machine at work and a machine at risk --- turns toward the new sound the way a turntable turns toward a distant noise.

And then, quietly, the third intelligence wakes. It lives in the office upstairs, where no operator has ever set foot. It has been handed the work that used to belong to a specific human: the one who noticed, the one who wrote the report, the one who rang the spare-parts supplier at eleven at night. It drafts the maintenance order. It checks the warehouse for the bearing. It composes the message to the production manager in the calm, plain language it has learned is hers. It does all of this before the sun has cleared the roof, and before any human being on the planet has decided that it should.

## The Human Who Is Not There

There is a person missing from this scene, and it is worth pausing on her, because she is the reason the scene matters. Until very recently, the maintenance order, the stock check, and the message to the production manager were *her* work. She was the one who noticed, on a morning like this one, that the bearing was complaining; the one who walked to the warehouse and found that the spare was not there; the one who rang the supplier at eleven at night and stayed late to draft the message. Her work was not unskilled. It required judgment, memory, and care, and it was the kind of work that a factory cannot run without.

And now, on this morning, the work is being done --- but she is not doing it. She is asleep, or she is on a train, or she is at home with her family, and the work is finishing itself in the quiet. This is the human fact at the centre of the whole book, and it is not a fact about replacement. It is a fact about *relocation*. The work she did has not vanished; it has been *lifted* --- off her shoulders, and onto the loop. What remains for her is the higher work: to check that the order is right, to decide whether the prediction should be trusted, to take responsibility for the answer. She has not lost her job. She has been promoted out of the eleven-o'clock-at-night phone call and into the judgment of whether the call was the right one.

That promotion is the sixth revolution, seen from the human side, and it is the side this book will not let you forget. The machines will do more and more of the *doing*. The human will do more and more of the *answering*. And the answering is the work that no machine can ever take, because it requires something at stake --- and only a human has something at stake.

## Not Science Fiction

This is not science fiction. It is the outline of an already-existing system, sketched from the projects a small lab publishes on a public repository, and it is the subject of the whole of this book. The arm, the sensor, the private cloud, the agent in the office --- these are not five separate miracles. They are five links in one chain, and the chain is the sixth industrial revolution.

I tell you the story from the inside because I have built parts of it, and I can tell you with an honesty that most vendors cannot: the individual pieces were already impressive. What was missing, what made this the sixth revolution rather than merely a very good fifth, was the *connection*. A machine that thinks but cannot speak to the cloud is a genius in a soundproof room. A cloud that keeps its secrets but cannot be reached by a machine is a vault with no door. An agent that works all night but was never told what the factory needs is a secretary with no memory. The revolution is not any one of these. The revolution is the moment they are finally plugged into one another.

So settle back, and watch the factory. In the coming pages we will learn how the arm came to think, why the cloud had to be built inside a wall, how the office came to keep its own hours, and what all of this means for the people who work, and will keep working, at the heart of it all.

The machine that dreamed is already dreaming. We are only going to learn its language.
