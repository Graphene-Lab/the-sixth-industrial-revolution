
# Chapter 2 --- What a Revolution Does to a Person

## The Coachman

In the early years of the automobile, there was a profession that had existed for a thousand years, and then there was a profession that did not. The coachmen, the postilions, the keepers of the relay stables --- these were not abstract "labor" in an economist's chart. They were men named Thomas, men who knew the difference between a tired horse and a sick one, men whose hands remembered the weight of a rein, men whose entire livelihood depended on a skill that the engine made obsolete before they had finished reading about it in the newspaper.

The story of the coachman is the story of every industrial revolution, told in the smallest units. When we speak of the "sixth industrial revolution" we tend to speak in the large and the abstract: GDP, productivity, the grand machinery of nations. But a revolution is not a large thing. A revolution is a very small thing happening to one person at an ordinary hour. It is a man or a woman discovering, on a Tuesday that began like any other, that the world they had spent thirty years mastering has quietly become a museum.

This is the human fact the economists quietly skip, and it is the fact this book must not skip. Every revolution of industry first *liberates* the human from a burden --- the crushing of muscle, the drudgery of repetition, the danger of the forge --- and then, almost as an afterthought, *relocates* the human to a new elevation. The coachman did not lose work and vanish; the economy simply made new kinds of work --- engines to maintain, roads to build, cities to spread along new radiating highways --- and pulled the human upward onto them. The transition was not gentle. It lasted generations. It produced a great deal of anxiety, and a great deal of writing about that anxiety.

## The Anxiety Is New Only in Its Subject

There is a particular kind of writing that every revolution spawns, and it always comes from the older generation, worried that the young will be ruined. In the fifth revolution's dawn, a Roman senator named Cato the Elder warned that the lathe and the wheel would corrupt the youth and ruin the empire. He was, of course, talking about machinery of his own day. The pattern is unmistakable and it has repeated without interruption: each new generation of machine produces a prophet of doom for the human spirit, and each generation of humans turns out, nonetheless, to have adapted to the machine and to have built a higher life upon it.

The current prophet worries that the machine will take the mind, not the muscle. This is the genuine novelty of the sixth revolution, and it deserves to be taken seriously. Earlier revolutions outsourced the body --- the steam engine lifted what the back could not, the tractor reaped what a dozen scythes could not, the forklift carried what four men could not. They freed the human for higher physical work, and then, as the body became less necessary, they raised the human toward the *mental*. The engineer, the accountant, the dispatcher, the manager --- these were the "higher" roles the body-labor freed humans climbed into. But those roles, too, were built on a premise that the sixth revolution now questions: the premise that judgment, planning, and the handling of information were uniquely, permanently human territory.

This is the first time a revolution is aimed directly at the mental life itself. And so the anxiety is reasonable, and it is not silly. But anxiety is a poor navigator, and this chapter is an attempt to hand you a better map.

## The Relocation Took Generations

Before we press on, it is worth admitting plainly how long and how uneven the earlier relocations really were, because a gentle summary would lie to you. The relocation of the coachman, or of the clerk, did not happen in a quarter-century the way a brochure suggests; it unfolded across *generations*, and it was spread across people with uneven hands. When the assembly line raised a worker's pay it did so while it quietly removed the pride of the whole, and it took a full century --- slow, unglamorous, and contested --- before society as a whole measured both richer and freer than before. Real wages and life expectancy, in the countries that industrialised first, rose dramatically; but they rose over roughly a hundred years of quiet struggle, not in a single shining moment. Some people were promoted almost immediately, on the new rungs; others waited decades, or never reached them at all.

There is a small, curious example that makes this concrete, and it is a recent one, so that you may be able to imagine it. For most of the twentieth century an ordinary profession still existed that we would no longer recognise: the elevator operator. The safety elevator had been invented by Elisha Otis in the middle of the nineteenth century, and the tall buildings followed; yet for more than a century the people who rode the lift still rode with a person who closed the doors and called the floors. The job was not removed overnight. It was phased out slowly, as the brakes and the safety devices became clever enough to drive the lift without a hand, and it was not until the early 1980s --- when cities such as New York repealed the rule requiring an operator --- that the profession finally ended, the last operators leaving the lift, for the last time, in the mid-1980s.

> **A curiosity.** The elevator operator is a reminder that the relocations this book describes did not stop with the steam engine or the computer. A person standing in a lift, handling the controls, was a real, paid, ubiquitous job well into the modern era --- and it, too, was quietly demoted to a machine, not in a single day but across fifty years. The sixth revolution is the latest wave of the same long process.

## What Actually Happened to the People Before Us

To understand where the sixth revolution is taking us, it helps to watch the earlier ones take their people, slowly and without malice. Let us trace the relocation once, carefully.

Before the assembly line, a car was built by a crew of skilled fitters who shared the work and took pride in the whole. Henry Ford did not merely invent the moving line; he *decomposed* the craft into small, repeatable motions and distributed them along a belt. The skilled fitter was displaced --- but so was the brute labor of carrying parts, because the belt carried them. What remained was a new kind of work: not crafting the whole, but tending a process; not making one car, but feeding a rhythm. The pay doubled, and the work was, to many, more exhausting and less proud. The relocation was not a pure blessing; it was a trade.

Then the computer arrived, and it displaced the *calculator* --- the roomful of human "computers" (mostly women) who solved ballistics and ship routing by hand, and later the mechanical calculators that ran the great offices. Here the relocation moved upward once more: the human was lifted from arithmetic into *interpretation*, into deciding what the numbers meant and what to do with them. The clerk became the analyst; the analyst became the planner.

Notice the recurring shape of the relocation. Each time a machine took over a *lower* task, the human was not simply removed; the human was *promoted* to the next available rung above. The muscle was automated, so the human did the mental. The mental arithmetic was automated, so the human did the interpretive. And the interpretive was being automated now, so the human would be promoted --- or displaced --- again.

## The Fifth Promotion, and Its Uncertainty

The sixth revolution is asking us to make a fifth promotion. If the machine judges, plans, and even acts, what is left for the human? The honest answer is that we are still finding out, and it is precisely this uncertainty --- this collective, unspoken question --- that defines the mood of the moment. It is a strange time to be a worker, because for the first time the worker is both the one being liberated and the one being questioned.

But there is comfort here as well as anxiety. The history of the earlier relocations is not a story of the machine winning and the human losing; it is a story of the human being raised, again and again, to a rung above the one the machine had just taken.

The sixth revolution will be the highest and most uncertain of these relocations, because it is the one that touches the seat of our distinctiveness most directly. The people who fare best will be those who learn, early, to stand beside the machine not as its rival but as its *conductor* --- the one who decides which of the machine's judgments are worth acting upon, who holds the authority to veto, and who does the human work that no machine, however intelligent, can do: take responsibility for the outcome.

## The People Who Adapted

To balance the anxiety, it is worth watching the other outcome --- the one that the optimists remember --- actually happen, in a real place, to real people. The historical record is full of communities and workers who were told they were finished, and who were not. When the power loom made hand-spun cloth impossible, the weavers who refused to adapt were indeed swept aside; but the ones who adapted did not simply survive, they *climbed*. A loom-tender became a machine-maintainer, who understood the mechanism better than any woven cloth; the maintenance became a trade, then a profession, then a career with its own schools and its own wages. The craft did not vanish; it *moved*, and the people who were willing to move with it moved upward on it.

There is a modern echo of this that you may be able to see. The arrival of the search engine, and then the recommendation algorithm, was widely taken to mean that the web researcher --- the person whose job was to find information and hand it back --- was finished. And yet the role did not disappear; it *promoted* into the data analyst, then the growth analyst, then the one who decides what the data means and what the business should do. The tool made the search instant, and the human was lifted from the *finding* of information to the *judging* of it. The machine did the lower work; the human did the higher work, and was paid for the higher work. This is the shape of the relocation, again and again: the machine takes the task *below*, and the human is raised to the task *above*, if the human is willing to be raised.

## The Fear Is Rational, but Not Fatal

It would be dishonest to promise that the anxiety is unfounded. The anxiety is rational, and it is not silly --- the previous relocations were real, and they were, on net, net-positive for the human condition, even though the transitions were brutal, unequal, and long. Every generation that "lost" its work ended up, a generation later, with more leisure, higher wages, and more novel forms of work than its grandparents could have imagined. But it is worth separating the two halves of the fear, because only one of them is likely to come true.

The first half is this: the tasks will change, and some current jobs will genuinely shrink or vanish. That part is certain, and it has been certain for two hundred years. The second half is a quieter, more tempting error --- the belief that because the tasks change, the *human* is now useless, and that the elevation which has raised every previous generation will not rise again. That second half is not supported by the record. Each previous relocation was, at the moment it happened, feared to be permanent and fatal, and each one turned out to be a step up, once the dust settled and the new system was built. The sixth revolution is the most uncertain of the relocations, because it touches the seat of our distinctiveness most directly. But it is, nonetheless, a relocation and not an extermination.

## The Ledger

Any honest accounting of a revolution must record both sides, and it is tempting to record only the comforting side --- the elevation, the rising game, the people who adapted. But the other side is also real, and it must be counted, or the accounting becomes propaganda rather than a balance sheet. The relocations were net-positive for the human condition *on average and over time* --- but "on average" and "over time" are doing a great deal of work in that sentence. On average, each relocation raised the floor; but *on average* also means that some people rose faster than others, and some never rose at all, and the ones who never rose paid the cost in full while the gains were spread thin. And *over time* means that the gain came to the *generation after*, not to the one that lived the transition. The man who lost his work to the assembly line did not feel the rising wages of his grandchildren; he felt the closing of the door, in his own lifetime.

It is worth placing one number beside the feeling, because numbers anchor the fear and keep it honest. It is estimated, from the long-run records of economic growth, that global per-capita output has risen roughly fifteen-fold since 1820 --- a figure that is almost too large to hold, because for ten thousand years before that date the curve had barely moved at all. In that single observation, compressed, is the entire argument for the relocations: the human condition did not merely improve; it changed *kind*, over a span of time short enough to fit inside a family lineage. But the ledger also records the other half of the truth, which is that the rise was *uneven* and *slow to arrive*, and that the people who did not rise with it were not living in a statistic --- they were living their life, in real time, waiting for a tide that had not yet reached their door.

This is the honest accounting: the revolution lifted everyone, eventually, while it left some people behind, immediately. The optimist is right that the water rose. The pessimist is right that it rose unevenly, and that some were already drowning when it reached their necks. Both are right, and the truth is the balance of the two. The sixth revolution is likely to do the same --- it will raise the floor and it will leave some behind --- and the moral task is not to insist that the rising water is all there is, nor that the drowning is all there is, but to build the system --- the private cloud, the controllable agent, the deterministic office --- that keeps the water from ever rising high enough to drown anyone who does not wish to swim.

## The Quiet Bargain

Every industrial revolution, in the end, makes a bargain with the human. It says: *I will take the work that is dangerous, or dreary, or simply too much for one set of hands, and in exchange I ask that you stop defining yourself by that work.* The coachman ceased to be a coachman and began to be something else. The clerk became an analyst. The operator became a conductor. The bargain is always generous if you accept it, and always frightening if you cling to the old name.

This is what the sixth revolution is asking, and it is the quiet thread beneath every chapter of this book. It is not asking you to be replaced. It is asking you to be *promoted* --- to the architect, to the conductor, to the one who decides. Whether that promotion feels like relief or like threat depends, in the end, less on the machine than on how quickly you are willing to let go of the old name.

## The Name You Will Keep

And so we return, quietly, to the promise that this book has carried from its very first page --- and which the projects behind these chapters exist to make real. The sixth revolution is not asking you to be replaced. It is asking you to be *promoted* --- to the architect, to the conductor, to the one who decides which of the machine's judgments are worth acting upon, who holds the authority to veto, and who does the human work that no machine can do: take responsibility for the outcome.

The coachman did not vanish; he became something else, and then something else again, across a century of quiet relocations. The clerk became an analyst, the analyst a planner, the planner a conductor. The front-end developer, writing interfaces by hand, may become the one who shapes the deterministic system that writes those interfaces --- as UISupportBlazor already does --- and tends the back-end logic while the machine tends the screen. Each time, the person kept the work and simply moved it one rung higher, standing beside the machine rather than competing with it. The relocations were not a zero-sum game in which the machine won and the human lost; they were a rising game in which the elevation of the human kept rising, even as the tasks below it kept falling to machines.

Whether that promotion feels like relief or like threat is, in the end, not a question about the machine at all. It is a question about how quickly you are willing to let go of the old name, and how soon you are ready to take on the new one. The machines are doing the lower work faster than any generation has ever seen. The only question that ever really mattered is whether the human who is left will accept the promotion, or clutch the old title and call it by its old name.
